# UK politics from 2015 to October 2026: background briefing

Compiled 21 October 2026 from general knowledge and current news sources. Figures marked "approx." are rounded. Check anything important before relying on it.

## 1. The road to the referendum (2015–2016)
- **May 2015 general election:** David Cameron's Conservatives won an unexpected majority with 330 of 650 seats (36.9%). Labour won 232 seats, the SNP took 56 of Scotland's 59 seats and the Liberal Democrats collapsed to 8. UKIP won 12.6% of the vote but only 1 seat.
- **September 2015:** Jeremy Corbyn was elected Labour leader on a left-wing platform.
- Cameron had promised an in/out referendum on EU membership. He negotiated a reform deal with the EU in February 2016 and campaigned for Remain.
- **23 June 2016 EU referendum:** Leave won 51.9% to Remain's 48.1%, on a 72% turnout. England and Wales voted Leave; Scotland (62% Remain), Northern Ireland (56% Remain) and London voted Remain. Older, non-graduate and small-town voters leaned Leave, while younger, graduate and big-city voters leaned Remain.
- Cameron resigned, and Theresa May became Prime Minister in July 2016.

## 2. Brexit deadlock (2017–2019)
- **March 2017:** May triggered Article 50.
- **June 2017 snap election:** the Conservatives lost their majority, winning 317 seats to Labour's 262, and governed with DUP support.
- May's withdrawal agreement, including the Northern Ireland "backstop", was defeated three times in Parliament, the first time by a record 230 votes in January 2019. Brexit was delayed twice.
- **May 2019:** Nigel Farage's new Brexit Party won the European Parliament elections. May resigned.
- **July 2019:** Boris Johnson became PM. In September his prorogation of Parliament was ruled unlawful by the Supreme Court. He agreed a new deal in October that moved checks into the Irish Sea.
- **December 2019 general election:** "Get Brexit Done". The Conservatives won 365 seats (43.6%) and an 80-seat majority. Many traditional Labour "Red Wall" seats in the North and Midlands turned Conservative, and Labour fell to 202 seats.

## 3. Brexit delivered and its aftermath (2020–2024)
- **31 January 2020:** the UK left the EU. The Trade and Cooperation Agreement was signed on 24 December 2020, and the transition period ended on 31 December 2020. Free movement ended and a points-based immigration system began.
- **COVID-19:** lockdowns ran from March 2020. Later the Partygate scandal over lockdown-breaking gatherings in Downing Street led Johnson to resign in July 2022.
- **Liz Truss (September–October 2022):** her unfunded "mini-budget" triggered a market crisis and a rise in mortgage rates, and she lasted 49 days.
- **Rishi Sunak (October 2022–July 2024):** he agreed the Windsor Framework on Northern Ireland trade in February 2023. Inflation peaked above 11% in late 2022 and a cost-of-living crisis followed. His Rwanda deportation plan and small-boat crossings dominated the immigration debate.
- Net migration hit record highs (approx. 900,000 in the year to mid-2023), driven by work and study visas rather than EU free movement. Many Leave voters saw this as a betrayal of "take back control".
- **June 2024:** Farage returned as leader of Reform UK.

## 4. Labour in power (2024–2026)
- **4 July 2024 general election:** Keir Starmer's Labour won a landslide of 411 seats, on only 33.7% of the vote. The Conservatives fell to 121 seats (23.7%), their worst ever result. The Liberal Democrats won 72, the SNP 9, Reform 5 (on 14.3% of the vote) and the Greens 4.
- **November 2024:** Kemi Badenoch became Conservative leader.
- Early unpopular decisions included cutting the winter fuel payment, raising employer National Insurance in the October 2024 budget, and ongoing small-boat crossings. Labour's polling fell quickly.
- **2025:** Reform UK led national polls for most of the year, at times above 30%. In the May 2025 local elections Reform won hundreds of council seats and took control of about 10 councils, and it won the Runcorn by-election by 6 votes. Starmer pursued a "reset" with the EU (a May 2025 summit on food/agri trade, defence and youth mobility talks) while ruling out rejoining the single market, customs union or EU. Deputy PM Angela Rayner resigned in September 2025. The Greens grew under new leader Zack Polanski, and a new left party linked to Jeremy Corbyn and Zarah Sultana launched.
- **March 2026:** Rupert Lowe, an MP formerly in Reform, registered **Restore Britain**, a hard-right party publicly backed by Elon Musk. Its funding and links to extremist donors have drawn press scrutiny.
- **7 May 2026 elections:**
  - *Scotland:* the SNP stayed largest with about 57–58 of 129 seats, and John Swinney remained First Minister. Labour and Reform won about 17 seats each, the Greens 15, the Conservatives 12 and the Lib Dems 10. Turnout was about 53%.
  - *Wales:* in a historic upset, Plaid Cymru won 43 of 96 seats (35.4%) and Reform 34 (29.3%), while Labour collapsed to 9. Labour First Minister Eluned Morgan lost her seat, and Rhun ap Iorwerth (Plaid) became First Minister of a minority government.
  - *England:* Labour suffered heavy local election losses.
- **June–July 2026:** after the May defeats Labour MPs demanded change. Starmer announced his resignation on 22 June. Andy Burnham, the former Mayor of Greater Manchester who had returned to Parliament through the Makerfield by-election, was the only candidate with enough nominations. He became Labour leader and Prime Minister around 17–20 July 2026, without a general election.

## 5. Where things stand (October 2026)
- **Westminster polls (averages, approx.):** Labour 28%, Conservatives 22%, Reform UK 22%, Lib Dems 10%, Greens 9%, Restore Britain 3–4%, SNP 2–3%. Labour has recovered under Burnham. Reform has fallen from a peak of about 35% to about 21–22%.
- **Reform's troubles:** a police investigation into alleged illegal donations, a parliamentary standards probe into a reported £5 million gift to Farage from crypto billionaire Christopher Harborne (both deny wrongdoing), allegations of racism against some members, and competition from Restore Britain on its right flank. Farage remains personally well known and popular with his base.
- **Conservatives:** Badenoch is still leader. She is pitching lower taxes and deregulation, has ruled out any pact with Reform, and is roughly level with Reform. One large YouGov projection put the Conservatives on 130 seats and Reform on 123.
- **Brexit opinion:** for several years polls have consistently shown more people saying Brexit was the wrong decision than the right one (approx. 55–60% "wrong" vs 30–35% "right" when undecideds are excluded). However, few voters rank rejoining as a top priority, and no major Westminster party proposes rejoining soon. The Lib Dems and Greens favour closer ties or a customs union.
- **Top public concerns:** cost of living, the NHS, immigration and small boats, housing, and the economy and taxes.
- The next general election is due by August 2029.

## Key actors for the simulation
- **Andy Burnham:** Labour PM. "King of the North", soft-left, devolution champion and pragmatic.
- **Keir Starmer:** former PM, now on the backbenches.
- **Kemi Badenoch:** Conservative leader. Combative and economically right-wing.
- **Nigel Farage:** Reform UK leader. Brexit campaigner, anti-immigration populist, under investigation.
- **Rupert Lowe:** Restore Britain leader. Hard right, backed by Musk.
- **Ed Davey:** Liberal Democrat leader. Pro-EU, focused on care and the NHS.
- **Zack Polanski:** Green leader. Left populist.
- **John Swinney (SNP)** and **Rhun ap Iorwerth (Plaid Cymru):** heads of the Scottish and Welsh governments, pushing for more devolution and independence.
- **Media:** BBC; the Telegraph, Daily Mail and GB News on the right; the Guardian and Mirror on the left; The Times and FT in the centre. Online voices on X/Twitter (including Elon Musk) and TikTok.
- **Voter groups:** older Leave-voting "Red Wall" towns, young urban Remain voters, Scottish independence supporters, Welsh voters who switched to Plaid and Reform, professional "Blue Wall" southern suburbs drifting to the Lib Dems, ethnic-minority communities, farmers, NHS workers, small-business owners.
